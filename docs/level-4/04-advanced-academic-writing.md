# 04 · Advanced Academic Writing

Academic writing in English follows conventions that differ sharply
from conversational or even business English: heavy use of hedging,
nominalization, formal citation of sources, and a strong preference
for passive or agentless constructions when describing methods or
general claims. This module covers those conventions directly.

## 1. Hedging and cautious claims

Academic writing rarely states findings as absolute fact — it hedges
the strength of a claim to match the actual strength of the evidence.

| Overclaimed | Appropriately hedged |
|---|---|
| "This proves that remote work increases productivity." | "This suggests that remote work may be associated with increased productivity." |
| "Social media causes anxiety." | "The evidence indicates a possible link between social media use and anxiety." |

| Hedging device | Example |
|---|---|
| Modal verbs | may, might, could, would appear to |
| Hedging verbs | suggest, indicate, imply, appear to |
| Hedging adverbs | arguably, potentially, largely |

## 2. Nominalization (turning verbs into nouns)

Academic register favors nominalized forms, which sound more formal
and let a sentence pack more information into fewer clauses.

| Verb-based (informal) | Nominalized (academic) |
|---|---|
| "We analyzed the data and found..." | "Analysis of the data revealed..." |
| "The company decided to expand." | "The company's decision to expand..." |
| "Researchers investigated whether..." | "The investigation into whether..." |

Overusing nominalization makes writing dense and hard to read — use
it selectively, mainly in topic sentences and summaries of method.

## 3. Citing sources and reporting others' claims

| Function | Phrase |
|---|---|
| Neutral reporting | "Smith (2021) argues that..." |
| Reporting with distance (you may disagree) | "Smith (2021) claims that..." |
| Agreeing with a source | "As Smith (2021) rightly notes..." |
| Synthesizing multiple sources | "Several studies (Smith, 2021; Lee, 2019) converge on the finding that..." |

## 4. Structuring an academic paragraph (topic-evidence-analysis-link)

| Part | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Topic sentence | States the paragraph's single main idea |
| Evidence | Data, quotation, or citation supporting the idea |
| Analysis | Explains *why* the evidence supports the idea — don't leave this implicit |
| Link | Connects back to the essay's overall argument or transitions to the next paragraph |

## 5. Worked example — a full academic paragraph

> "Remote work arrangements appear to have a measurable, if modest,
> effect on employee retention. A 2022 survey of over 3,000
> knowledge workers found that respondents with flexible location
> policies were 18% less likely to report an intention to leave
> within the next year (Chen & Patel, 2022). This suggests that
> flexibility itself, rather than compensation alone, functions as a
> retention lever — a finding that complicates compensation-centric
> models of turnover. Accordingly, the following section considers
> whether this effect holds across seniority levels."

## 6. Cheat sheet — academic writing checklist

| Check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Did I hedge claims to match the actual strength of the evidence? | Overclaiming is one of the most common academic writing errors |
| Did I include analysis after evidence, not just evidence alone? | Unexplained evidence reads as unfinished argument |
| Did I vary how I introduce cited sources (argues/claims/notes)? | Repetitive citation verbs read as unpolished |
| Does each paragraph have one clear topic sentence? | Multi-topic paragraphs weaken argument structure |

## Exercise

Take a strong opinion you hold and write one academic-style paragraph
defending it, following the topic-evidence-analysis-link structure.
Include at least two hedging devices and one nominalized phrase.
Then rewrite the same paragraph in plain conversational English and
compare the register shift.

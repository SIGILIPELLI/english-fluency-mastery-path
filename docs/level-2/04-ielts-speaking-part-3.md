# 04 · IELTS Speaking Part 3

Part 3 follows immediately after Part 2 and lasts 4-5 minutes. The
examiner asks broader, more abstract discussion questions connected to
the Part 2 topic — asking for your opinions, comparisons, and
predictions rather than personal facts. This is where higher-band
candidates separate themselves: Part 3 explicitly rewards the ability
to analyze, compare, and speculate in English, not just describe.

## 1. How Part 3 differs from Parts 1 and 2

| Feature | Part 1 | Part 2 | Part 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question type | Personal, factual | Single described topic | Abstract, opinion-based |
| Answer length | 2-4 sentences | 1-2 minute monologue | Extended answers, examiner follows up |
| Focus | You, your life | A specific memory/experience | Society, trends, general truths |
| Example question | "Do you like reading?" | "Describe a book you enjoyed." | "Do you think fewer people read books nowadays than in the past? Why?" |

## 2. The question types to expect

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Comparison (past vs. present) | "How has the way people learn skills changed in the last twenty years?" |
| Cause and effect | "Why do you think some people struggle to learn new skills as adults?" |
| Speculation about the future | "Do you think technology will make certain skills unnecessary in future?" |
| Society-wide opinion | "Should schools focus more on practical skills than academic subjects?" |
| Comparing groups | "Do you think men and women learn skills differently?" |

## 3. A framework for extended opinion answers

A weak Part 3 answer is a one-line opinion. A strong one takes a clear
position and supports it with reasoning and an example — similar in
shape to a mini persuasive paragraph.

1. **State your opinion clearly** — "I'd say...", "In my view...",
   "I think it really depends, but generally..."
2. **Give a reason** — "mainly because...", "the main factor is..."
3. **Give a concrete example or illustration** — "for instance...",
   "you can see this with..."
4. **Acknowledge the other side (optional but raises band)** —
   "That said, some people would argue...", "Of course, it's not true
   for everyone..."

## 4. Worked example — a full Part 3 exchange

> **Examiner:** Do you think fewer people read books nowadays than in
> the past?
>
> **You:** I think it really depends on how you define "reading" —
> people probably read less in the traditional, sit-down-with-a-novel
> sense, but they're reading constantly on their phones: articles,
> messages, social media posts. So overall reading volume might not
> have dropped that much, it's just shifted format.
>
> **Examiner:** Why do you think some people struggle to learn new
> skills as adults?
>
> **You:** I'd say the biggest factor is time — as an adult you've
> usually got work and family responsibilities eating into your day, so
> there's just less room for slow, consistent practice, which is really
> what skill-learning needs. There's also a psychological side to it:
> adults are often more afraid of looking incompetent than children
> are, so they avoid practicing in front of others, which actually
> slows them down.
>
> **Examiner:** Do you think technology will make certain skills
> unnecessary in the future?
>
> **You:** Almost certainly, yes — you can already see it happening
> with things like handwriting or mental arithmetic, which used to be
> essential and now matter much less because of computers and
> calculators. That said, I don't think it's all negative; it usually
> just shifts what counts as a valuable skill, rather than making
> people less capable overall.

Each answer takes a position, gives reasoning, and includes a concrete
example — the pattern that examiners associate with Band 7+ responses.

## 5. Cheat sheet — Part 3 checklist

| Check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Did I give a clear position, not just "maybe, I don't know"? | Vague non-answers cap your Fluency and Coherence score |
| Did I support my opinion with a reason? | Shows Grammatical Range through complex, reasoned sentences |
| Did I include at least one concrete example? | Distinguishes strong answers from generic ones |
| Did I use hedging language appropriately ("tends to," "arguably")? | Natural for abstract topics — shows nuanced Lexical Resource |
| Did I avoid memorized "template" phrases that don't fit the question? | Examiners are trained to spot rehearsed, irrelevant answers |

## Exercise

Take the five question types from section 2 and write one original
question for each, based on a Part 2 topic of your choice. Answer each
one aloud using the four-step framework in section 3, aiming for 30-45
seconds per answer. Record yourself and check whether you gave a clear
position and a concrete example in every answer.
